Paul M. Gorny

Postdoctoral Researcher · Karlsruhe Institute of Technology

Welcome! I am a postdoctoral researcher (Akademischer Rat auf Zeit) at the Chair of Human Resource Management at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT). I obtained my PhD from the University of East Anglia and my bachelor's and research track master's degree from the Cologne Graduate School (CGS) at the University of Cologne.

I am a behavioral and experimental economist whose work examines how both gendered language and emerging technologies shape economic behavior and human interaction. In my job market paper I study how gender embedded in language, for example in job advertisements, influences perceptions and decisions surrounding hiring and applications. A second research stream investigates how emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and robots reshape human behavior, particularly within human-human interaction and team collaboration settings.

I am on the 2026/27 academic job market.

My research relies primarily on laboratory and field experiments as well as randomized controlled trials, and I complement these with projects using administrative, platform, and textual data. I am strongly committed to methodological innovation, integrating tools such as eye-tracking, biosensors, and advanced computational methods to deepen behavioral insights.

During my PhD, I worked on contest theory and networks with a particular focus on studies that apply to conflict, identity, and asymmetries in skills, prize valuations, and costs.

Working Papers

The gender of opportunity: How gendered job titles affect job seeker attraction
joint with Petra Nieken and Martin Trenkle · Job Market Paper
Online Appendix 
@unpublished{gorny2026gender,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Nieken, Petra and Trenkle, Martin},
  title  = {The Gender of Opportunity: How Gendered Job Titles Affect Job Seeker Attraction},
  note   = {Job market paper},
  year   = {2026}
}
Recruitment signals shape applicant pools. Across three studies, we examine whether gender-fair (vs. generic masculine) German job titles affect application behavior and job seekers' beliefs about organizational culture. In a large-scale preregistered field experiment, gender-fair titles increase female applications and clicks by about 50% in Business & Management, where priors are flexible. There is no average effect in categories with less flexible priors, consistent with a signaling model. Eye-tracking evidence points to belief updating rather than visual salience, and an online study supports gender-fair language as a credible equality signal. We find no evidence of male backlash.
The AI agentic penalty in interpersonal trust
joint with Emike Nasamu and Mengjie Wang
@unpublished{gorny2026agentic,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Nasamu, Emike and Wang, Mengjie},
  title  = {The {AI} Agentic Penalty in Interpersonal Trust},
  note   = {Working paper},
  year   = {2026}
}
AI can inform decisions about whom to trust, recommend an action, or initiate it for a person to confirm. Does allocating initiative to AI change how people use its information? We develop an AI tool using decisions from earlier trust experiments and study its use in a preregistered experiment with 1,596 participants. Participants decide whether to transfer money to another person, who can return part of it or keep it all. The AI predicts that person's behaviour, recommends a choice, or presets the choice for confirmation or costless override. The information, available actions, and final authority are the same in every role. Prediction and advice increase the share of choices matching the AI's assessment by about 34 percentage points over a baseline without access to it. When the AI takes the initiative, this share is 8–9 percentage points lower than under prediction or advice, forfeiting about a quarter of this influence. This AI agentic penalty is symmetric: participants respond less to both encouragements to trust and warnings against trusting. Beliefs also respond less to the assessment. Allocating initiative thus changes reliance on AI in interpersonal trust even when people retain final authority.
Adaptivity and revealed robot aversion in human-robot collaboration: A field-in-the-lab experiment
joint with Louis Schäfer · R&R at Experimental Economics 
@unpublished{gorny2025adaptivity,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Sch{\"a}fer, Louis},
  title  = {Adaptivity and Revealed Robot Aversion in Human-Robot Collaboration: A Field-in-the-Lab Experiment},
  note   = {MPRA Paper No. 126663},
  year   = {2025},
  url    = {https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/126663/}
}
We study human-robot collaboration in a controlled experiment run in a realistic production environment. Participants completed a sequential task in pairs, where one worker decided whether to pass intermediate components to a coworker or not. We find strong evidence of robot aversion: workers were significantly less likely to pass intermediate products in the robotic treatments. Adaptivity only marginally affected these outcomes. Our results demonstrate that cooperation and responsibility attribution in hybrid teams depend not only on performance but also on social perceptions of artificial agents.
Predicting generosity: The impact of personalized AI and human oversight on altruistic behavior
joint with Eva Groos and Christina Strobel · R&R at Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics 
@unpublished{gorny2025generosity,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Groos, Eva and Strobel, Christina},
  title  = {Predicting Generosity: The Impact of Personalized {AI} and Human Oversight on Altruistic Behavior},
  note   = {SSRN Working Paper},
  year   = {2025},
  doi    = {10.2139/ssrn.5713116}
}
We study how personalized AI predictions affect individual choices in the context of altruistic decisions. Using data from dictator games, we trained an AI tool to generate personalized predictions of dictators' choices. We find that participants sent less when they received predictions, with the strongest reduction when a human overseer withheld the prediction. These findings highlight the need to consider human reactions to AI predictions when assessing both the accuracy and impact of such tools.
Gendered language, economic behavior, and norm compliance
joint with Petra Nieken and Karoline Ströhlein · under review 
@unpublished{gorny2025gendered,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Nieken, Petra and Str{\"o}hlein, Karoline},
  title  = {Gendered Language, Economic Behavior, and Norm Compliance},
  note   = {Working paper. Previously circulated as CESifo Working Papers No. 10458 and 10459 (2023)},
  year   = {2025}
}
We conducted a controlled experiment to examine how the gender frame of instructions (male, female, or gender-inclusive), along with norm salience, influences norm compliance. We find a statistically significant negative effect of the gender-inclusive frame on norm compliance for almost all participants and a negative effect of the male gender frame on men's norm compliance. These findings contribute to the debate on gender-inclusive language by highlighting its unintended behavioral effects.
Previously circulated as The effects of gendered language on norm compliance and He, she, they? The impact of gendered language on economic behavior.
Information timing and switching costs: Evidence from a field-in-the-lab experiment
joint with Magnus Kandler, Gisela Lanza, Petra Nieken, and Karoline Ströhlein · under review 
@unpublished{gorny2026information,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Kandler, Magnus and Lanza, Gisela and Nieken, Petra and Str{\"o}hlein, Karoline},
  title  = {Information Timing and Switching Costs: Evidence from a Field-in-the-Lab Experiment},
  note   = {Working paper},
  year   = {2026}
}
Firms increasingly rely on digital technologies that generate real-time data, raising the question of how information should be provided to workers. We study how the timing and discretion of information provision affect productivity in a field-in-the-lab experiment conducted in a learning factory that mirrors key features of modern manufacturing environments. Participants perform a multitasking real-effort task assembling two product variants and decide when to retool a machine. We compare whether operational information is unavailable (Baseline), automatically pushed (Push), or pulled on demand (Pull). Information provision affects intertemporal coordination, particularly variant-switch timing. Pull increases output for infrequent switchers but reduces output for frequent switchers. Overall, conditional on switching, Pull outperforms Baseline, while Push yields no productivity gains. Our findings highlight a trade-off: discretion over information timing can facilitate strategic coordination but may reduce performance under frequent task switching.
Don't fear the robots: Automatability and job satisfaction
joint with Ritchie C. Woodard 
@unpublished{gorny2020robots,
  author = {Gorny, Paul M. and Woodard, Ritchie C.},
  title  = {Don't Fear the Robots: Automatability and Job Satisfaction},
  note   = {MPRA Paper No. 103424},
  year   = {2020},
  url    = {https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/103424/}
}
We analyse the correlation between job satisfaction and automatability. Using multiple survey datasets matched with various measures of automatability, we find a negative and statistically significant correlation that is robust to controlling for worker and job characteristics. We provide evidence that monotonicity and low perceived meaning of the job drive both automatability and low job satisfaction.
